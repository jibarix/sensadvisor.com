Building Agents Is Still Mostly Software Engineering

A Carnegie Mellon program on agentic AI confirmed it, and I compared what it taught with 18 other university courses.

J. A. Arroyo · SENS ADVISOR

The claim

Building an agent that works in production is still mostly software engineering. The model is one part. The hard work is the system around it, which has to stay reliable when the model isn’t.

I recently finished Carnegie Mellon University’s Agentic AI program, Building Autonomous Systems for Real-World Applications, offered by its School of Computer Science Executive Education, and it confirmed that view. Applied AI is still engineering: the models keep getting better, but whether an agent works for a business depends on everything built around it.

What the program taught me

  • Design the safeguards in from the start. Evaluation, guardrails, monitoring and human review are part of the design, not something added after deployment, and a good design sends only the right cases to a person.
  • Check a model’s confidence before trusting it. Before choosing a routing threshold, check how often the model is right at each confidence level.
  • Weigh one agent against several. One agent is simpler and easier to manage; several add coordination, latency and complexity, and they have to earn it.
  • Keep what the model sees apart from what the system keeps. The model works from selected history; the system keeps the full record, saves the state it needs to resume, and guards against duplicate effects when it retries a write.
  • Choose what the model sees. Decide when to retrieve and how much to pass to the model. Extra material can add noise and cost.
  • Design tools for the model. The model knows a tool only by its name, description, inputs and results, and every tool description costs context.
  • Treat security as a system problem. Threats run through memory, orchestration, the runtime and the inputs, so the answer is defence in depth, not a single fix.
  • Don’t mistake a benchmark score for readiness. Evaluation also covers calibration, robustness, cost and bias, and after a change of model you test the known failures again.

How it lines up with other courses

Alongside the program, I have been documenting how universities teach this. My guide, Learning to Build Agentic Systems, brings together the public materials of 18 university courses on AI agents and agentic systems from 2025 and 2026, from hands-on engineering courses to research seminars, at universities including Carnegie Mellon, Stanford, UC Berkeley, Michigan and Harvard. The program I took is not one of them, which makes the comparison useful.

  • Choosing what the model sees is taught in nearly every course.
  • Security as a system problem and designing tools for the model are taught in several, some in depth.
  • Building evaluation and review into the system, tracking state and retries, and testing failures again after a change are taught in parts across many courses. I didn’t find one that puts them together the way the program did.
  • One agent or several is an open question: some courses reward a single agent that matches a team at lower cost. Others report teams of agents doing better on long tasks, at a higher cost. The program also taught it as a trade-off.
  • Checking a model’s confidence before routing on it is where the program stood out: I didn’t find it taught as a practice in the other courses.

What made the program worth it

The best part was the people. The program had a lot of live interaction with the professor and the learning facilitators, an incredible group of intelligent people at the disposal of every participant, and the program’s support team was there in the sessions too, taking care of the logistics. Everything was clearly explained, not only in the extra time they gave to answering questions in the live sessions, labs and office hours, but also on the learning platform.

Would I recommend it?

Yes, highly. I recommend it to anyone who wants to learn how agents are built, to shape their own thinking about them, to get up to date, or simply to get real feedback from real professionals and academics.

Source and limits

What the program taught is paraphrased from its course materials and sessions and from my own experience in it. The comparison with other courses comes from my guide’s course tables: a course counts as teaching a point when a lecture, lab, reading or stated objective teaches it, and where I say I didn’t find something, I mean in the tables I compiled. The guide is my own; no university has reviewed or endorsed it.
