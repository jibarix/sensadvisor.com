What Adopting Companies Report

Anthropic's interviews with companies already using AI, read beside the requirements this series tracks.

J. A. Arroyo · SENS ADVISOR

This series reads the requirements businesses publish when they ask for AI systems to be built, fixed or extended. On 23 November 2026, Anthropic's economics team published Working with AI, by Nathan Wilmers, Maxim Massenkoff and Peter McCrory, which hears from the other end: 66 interviews with people at 58 US companies, all Anthropic enterprise customers, that are already deploying AI. Its findings on where value comes from, what happens to jobs and what holds deployments back say a good deal about what requirements should settle in advance. This note summarises the study and sets it beside the November edition.

Reading the study

These are interviews, not a survey. The companies were chosen for variety across industry, size and AI use, not to represent the US economy or even Anthropic's customers. The value of each use was the interviewee's own description, “not typically based on A/B tests or other quantitative assessments”, and an automated coder classified uses, software and bottlenecks from the transcripts. The authors present the work as patterns for later quantitative research to test.

Value comes mostly from more output

The authors coded nearly 300 applications, each a specific use of AI at a specific company. Among the applications whose benefits interviewees described, most added value by increasing output rather than by cutting costs. About half of those output-increasing applications accelerated or expanded existing work, and the other half improved products and services or created new ones: an up-front estimate of a patient's cost after insurance, an after-hours answering service at a dental company, mock-ups of vacant units fitted out in a prospective tenant's branding. In about a tenth of the applications for which interviewees described the benefits, employees captured the value directly, as more enjoyable work or simply less work.

Light-touch uses such as ad hoc data analysis or slide design fit into existing structures, but interviewees often rated them as low value. The higher-value uses tended to require changes to processes or roles, such as AI review of every code commit, or a marketing manager who maintains branding guidelines for the AI instead of designing each document.

Few layoffs, less hiring, fewer contractors

People at five companies described pushing out employees who would not adopt AI, and only two companies had seen automation fully eliminate a position and lead to a job loss. Ten reported hiring more because of AI, mostly into implementation and IT support roles. Reduced hiring was far more widely reported: at two-thirds of the companies, people said they were hiring less because of AI. A third of those declines came from shrinking functions through attrition; most were hiring the company would otherwise have needed in order to grow. As one engineering leader put it, “We've added a lot of capacity by buying tokens instead of hiring people.”

Nine companies cut contracted developers, customer service, legal counsel or back-office work, much of it already offshored; one finance company let go of well over 100 offshore data and developer contractors. The authors suggest these cuts may be an early warning for international business services. One company's document-review agent led it to cut the number of reviewers it expected to hire for the coming year by more than 90%. The six AI-native startups that gave an estimate said they would need, on average, four times as many employees without AI.

Top-down automations can be brittle

Many companies start by choosing a workflow and automating it from the top. Companies often attributed their contractor cuts to this kind of automation, and top-down deployments could also be fragile. Many were built by outside vendors as targeted products for one industry and function, and improving models soon left them out of date: “It'll be obsolete in three months.” A law firm ended up with lightly used “zombie” apps; a customer service scoring system ran on a two-year-old model that managers no longer took seriously.

Expert work adds a further problem: the experts who must design the prompts and judge the outputs are being asked to build what could replace them. At the company with the review agent, management wanted reviewers to double their output and met resistance; some reviewers were down to two minutes per submission, others still took ten or more. At a law firm, an agent for conflict-of-interest checks shifted hard cases onto the conflicts staff, who pushed back, and the firm returned to its old process.

Bottom-up building stays in pockets

The alternative, handing out tools and waiting, produced some striking individual results. An accountant built an app to audit whether employers' 401(k) payments reach the right fund, work that had taken 80 hours; quality assurance supervisors at a chemical plant with no coding experience built an app to monitor filtration rates. Across all 140 pieces of AI-built software that interviewees described, product features and infrastructure stayed with technical staff, while nontechnical staff built internal apps and dashboards. The authors infer that most software built by nondevelopers still depends on technical staff for support or infrastructure.

Such projects stay local, need maintenance, and can create work elsewhere: the 401(k) prototype needed a third-party vendor to implement it and was still not in active use months later. Recurring bottlenecks across the interviews included reviewing AI output, uneven acceleration between tasks, uneven adoption between people, and missing context and infrastructure. Customer scepticism and regulation were rarely cited; most bottlenecks came from inside the organisation.

Reorganising around AI

The applications interviewees rated highest in value tended to involve substantial organisational change. The authors describe three ways some companies have reorganised.

  • Broader roles. One person or a small team owns a whole workflow or customer relationship, which cuts the handoffs and vetoes that slow everything else down; one executive described merging lead sourcing, account executive and customer success into a single role.
  • Redeploying people to the bottleneck tasks. Engineers move toward decisions, review and talking to users; project managers, freed from morning calls to site crews, spend the time with customers. Code review became a bottleneck in its own right.
  • Common infrastructure. Shared, consolidated data that agents and employees can query directly, and a safe place for nondevelopers' apps to run. A trucking company's agent-accessible data warehouse replaced queues for analysis that could run “a year or two”.

The authors' conclusion is that, at current model capabilities, experts remain essential to building and reviewing AI workflows, and that employers who redeploy people and broaden roles may be best placed to gain from AI without displacing employees. They add that this guarantees nothing about job loss.

Beside the requirements

The two sources look at different populations at different stages, so neither tests the other. Read together, they suggest where requirements could do more.

Checks. Interviewees usually described value qualitatively, and reviewing AI output came up repeatedly as a bottleneck. In the November edition, 46% of projects state no success check. For a project brief, I would agree representative cases and acceptance criteria before building, so that review becomes a defined check rather than open-ended reading.

Maintenance and ownership. The interviews show what ongoing maintenance can involve: one engineer read every transcript of his alert agent for two months and still sampled them every two weeks six months on. My recommendation is that requirements name who will do that work once the system is live, and who handles its exceptions.

Model changes can date implementations. Among projects in this series that ask for an “agent”, a fixed workflow is the most common design their requirements describe (41% in November), and where it meets the acceptance criteria it is often the right one. Some interviewees described systems they used becoming outdated as models improved. That suggests recording which steps are business requirements and which can be revisited when the model changes.

The organisation is part of the scope. Most bottlenecks the adopters named were inside their own organisations. The conflict-checking workflow shifted difficult cases from attorneys to the conflicts staff, who pushed back. For a brief, I would identify who inherits that kind of work and check with them that the transfer is workable.

Source and limits

Nathan Wilmers, Maxim Massenkoff and Peter McCrory, Working with AI, Anthropic, 23 November 2026, with a PDF that includes an appendix on method. Study numbers and quotations come from the report's text, and the comparison figures from the November edition. The company examples are as the interviewees described them, and the interviews were anonymous.

All 66 interviews were single video calls of 25 to 40 minutes. Only some of the companies approached agreed to take part. Against AI-adopting firms in the Census Bureau's Business Trends and Outlook Survey, the authors report that their companies are larger, skew toward software and finance, and underrepresent retail and transportation. All the companies are customers of the company that published the study. This note quotes only numbers stated in the report's text, not values read from its charts.
